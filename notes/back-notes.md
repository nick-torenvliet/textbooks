# Sitting vs. Hiking — Notes on Sciatica & Stenosis

Not medical advice, but the pattern is common and physiologically straightforward.

## Why hiking helps stenosis + sciatica

- Walking, especially on uneven terrain or uphill, keeps the lumbar spine in slight *flexion*, which opens the spinal canal and the neural foramina — directly relieving the mechanical compression behind stenosis-driven sciatic symptoms.
- Continuous gait engages the glutes, deep core, and hip extensors, which take load off the lumbar discs and stabilize the pelvis.
- Constant motion keeps nerve roots gliding rather than tethered, and improves perfusion to the discs (they have no direct blood supply — they pump nutrients in/out via movement).

## Why sitting brings it back

- Seated lumbar disc pressure is roughly 40% higher than standing — and the L4/L5 and L5/S1 levels (where stenosis and sciatic compression usually live) bear most of it.
- Sitting puts the hip flexors (psoas, rectus femoris) in a shortened position for hours. When you stand up, they pull the pelvis into anterior tilt, exaggerate lumbar lordosis, and close the foramina back down.
- Glutes go dormant ("gluteal amnesia"), so the lumbar spine and piriformis pick up the slack — piriformis tightening is a classic sciatic trigger.
- Static loading also starves the discs of the nutrient exchange they got from walking.

## Practical levers that usually work

- A sit/stand desk, or a hard rule like "stand or walk 5 min every 30."
- Daily walking volume — try to keep some fraction of the hiking dose. Even 30–45 min/day matters.
- Hip flexor stretching + glute activation (bridges, clamshells) to undo the sitting posture.
- For stenosis specifically, flexion-biased movement (e.g., knees-to-chest, child's pose) tends to feel better than extension.

## When to escalate

If symptoms are pronounced and recurring, worth a physiatrist or PT visit — they can tell you whether it's primarily stenosis, disc, or piriformis driving it, since the treatment emphasis differs.
